Could the wholeuniverse be acomputation?

Stephen Wolfram is a physicist and software maker. His bet: underneath everything, the universe runs a tiny rule, over and over. Space, time, gravity and the odd behaviour of tiny particles would all grow out of that one rule.

A computation is just that: a rule, applied step by step. Nothing more mysterious.

This page lets you play with the pieces of that idea, one scene at a time. Each scene ends with a guess. Make it before you read the answer; that is where the learning happens.

A hypothesis, not settled physics. Wolfram's picture is a serious, detailed proposal, but it has not yet made a prediction that an experiment has checked. Wherever this page describes one of his specific claims, you will see this tag: Wolfram's idea

Everything without the tag is standard science you would find in a textbook.

Make a guess

If the universe really runs on a rule, how long is the rule?

Wolfram's idea A few lines, maybe less. His reason is the whole of the next scene: tiny rules can make enormous complexity. Nobody has found the rule, so for now this is a hunch, not a fact. Hold your own guess in mind as you go.

Scene 2

Simple rules, wild results

Here is the simplest kind of computation there is.

A row of cells. Each cell is either filled or empty. On every step, each cell looks at itself and its two neighbours, and a rule says whether it will be filled next. Rows pile up downward, so the picture is a history: the top row is the start, and each row below is the next step.

This toy is called a cellular automaton: cells, plus an automatic rule. Only one thing to learn before you play: the rule has exactly eight cases, one for each way three cells can look.

The rule Eight cases, read from 111 down to 000 (left to right, then the next row on a phone). Tap an output, the square underneath, to flip it.

Rule 30 00011110 Read the outputs left to right as a binary number and you get the rule's name. 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 256 possible rules.

Press Step one cell to watch a single cell get computed, or Run to see the whole pattern.

Challenge Rule 72 kills the lone cell. Flip one case to keep it alive.

Then tap one output square in the rule above and watch the pattern.

Where this comes from

John von Neumann and Stanisław Ulam invented cellular automata in the 1940s. John Conway's Game of Life made them famous in 1970. In the early 1980s Wolfram did something new: he studied the very simplest ones, all 256, systematically, and found that even these can look random or build complex structures. Rule 110 is the star: in 2004 Matthew Cook published a proof that it can run any program at all.

One more thing. The textile cone snail grows a Rule 30-like pattern on its shell. Nature seems to run little rules too.

Scene 3

Drop the grid: space as a network

A grid of cells cheats.

It assumes space already exists (the cells sit in a row) and that a clock ticks (one row per step). Wolfram wants both to come out of the rule, not go into it.

So he keeps only two things: dots, and links between dots. A dot has no address and no coordinates. Its "location" is just which other dots it is linked to.

He calls the whole thing a hypergraph, a network where one link can join more than two dots. Our links join two dots, which is enough to see the idea.

The rule Find two links leaving the same dot. Replace them with four links and one brand-new dot.

Each press finds every place the rule fits (without overlaps) and rewrites them all at once.

Each rewrite is an event. In this picture, time is nothing more than events piling up. There is no master clock. The button is only a convenience for you: nothing in the network knows how fast you press it, or whether two rewrites happened "at the same time".

Wolfram's idea The real universe would hold something like 10400 of these dots, which he calls atoms of space. That is a 1 followed by 400 zeros. Everything you can see, including you, is a pattern in how they are linked.

Make a guess

With no grid and no ruler, how would you measure the distance between two dots?

Count links. The fewest links you can walk from one dot to another is the distance between them. Where the dots are drawn is only our layout, chosen to look tidy; the network itself knows nothing about it.

Scene 4

From distance to dimension, curvature and gravity

Once you can measure distance by counting links, you can measure a surprising amount.

A. How many dimensions?

Pick a dot. Grow a ball around it: every dot within r links. Count what is inside. On a line, the count grows in step with r. On a flat grid it grows like r × r. In a 3D lattice, like r × r × r. The power is the dimension. So a network can have a dimension even though nobody ever placed it in a space.

Tap a dot to pick it. Arrow keys move it; drag to spin the 3D lattice.

B. Curved or flat?

Now a different test, on three meshes made of triangles. Where 5 triangles meet at every corner, the surface has to curl up into a ball (an icosahedron). With 6 it lies flat. With 7 there is too much material at every corner, so it ruffles into a saddle, like the edge of a lettuce leaf.

Same counting as before: grow a ball of radius r from the middle dot. The curled surface holds fewer dots than flat; the ruffled one holds more. That gap, compared with flat, is curvature, measured by counting.

Make a guess

On the ball, will a radius-3 ball hold more dots or fewer than on the flat mesh?

Fewer: 12 against 37. On the icosahedron a radius of 3 already reaches the far side, so there is simply nothing more to count.

The Earth check. Walk 10,000 km south from the North Pole and you reach the equator, which is about 40,000 km around. A flat circle with a 10,000 km radius would be about 63,000 km around. The Earth "holds less" than flat. That shortfall is its curvature, and you could measure it without ever leaving the ground.

C. Why curvature is gravity

Two walkers stand on the equator, one mile apart. Both walk due north, in perfectly straight lines, never turning left or right.

Make a guess

What happens to the gap between them?

It shrinks, and they meet at the North Pole. Neither walker ever turned. The ground itself is curved, so straight paths drift together. Watch it happen:

0°

Gap: 1.00 mile

The gap is drawn far wider than a mile so you can see it. The rule is exact though: at any latitude the gap is the starting gap times the cosine of the latitude, which reaches zero at the pole.

This is Einstein's picture of gravity. Things do not get "pulled". They follow the straightest possible path through curved spacetime, space and time taken together. For a falling apple, most of the curve is in time rather than space, which is why the effect feels like a pull toward the ground instead of a drift sideways.

Wolfram's idea The ball-count mismatch in his network is the very curvature that sits in Einstein's equations. Energy is the amount of rewriting activity in a region, and activity curves the network, so matter bends space the way Einstein said. He has shown this works inside the model's own mathematics, under assumptions about what a typical huge network looks like.

The test he hopes for: the number of dimensions of space might fluctuate very slightly from place to place or time to time. Nobody has detected such a thing.

Scene 5

Branching histories and quantum mechanics

Back in scene 3, the rule fitted in several places at once, and we rewrote them all. But often the places overlap, and then you must choose which goes first. Different choices give different networks.

Wolfram's idea The universe does not choose. It takes every option, so history branches. Unlike the popular "many worlds" picture, branches here can also merge again, whenever different orders arrive at the same state.

You can watch this with letters instead of networks. Two rules: A → AB and B → A. Each step, replace exactly one letter. Start from A. The map of every possible history is called a multiway graph.

Generation 0: one state, A. Press Next generation.

gold ring + ×2: reached from two different earlier states (a merge)Tap any state to light up every path that leads to it.

Make a guess

If every branch really happens, why do we only ever experience one history?

Wolfram's idea We are enormous compared with the network, and we are spread across many nearby branches at once. Our brains stitch them into a single story. Nothing tells us where we sit in the map of branches (he calls that map branchial space), so individual outcomes look random. That, he says, is the randomness of quantum mechanics.

And everyone agrees on results for the same reason everyone on Earth sees the same night sky: we all sit close together in branchial space. It is a story with mathematics behind it, not yet a tested prediction.

Scene 6

Spooky action and the speed limit

First, the established science.

Make two particles together, send them far apart, and measure them. Their answers match far too reliably to be luck. Physicists call the pair entangled. Experiments since the 1970s have ruled out the obvious excuse, that the particles agreed on their answers in advance; the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to three of the people who showed it. Einstein called the whole business spooky action at a distance.

Wolfram's idea The two particles share an ancestor in the branch map. So they are neighbours in branchial space even when they are far apart in ordinary space. Nothing travels between them; in the map, they were never really separate.

Here is the part that trips most people up. Try it.

Make a guess

Could you text your friend faster than light this way?

No. You cannot choose how your coins land, so you cannot put a message into them. Your friend sees pure noise. The perfect match only shows up when you compare notes, and that comparison travels the slow way. Physicists call this the no-communication theorem, and it holds in quantum mechanics and in Wolfram's picture alike.

Footnote. Matching coins alone could be faked with pre-agreed answers, like a pair of gloves split into two boxes. The real quantum twist, shown by Bell tests, is subtler: the particles' answers to several different questions match in a pattern that no pre-agreed list can reproduce. That is what the Nobel-winning experiments measured.

Scene 7

Who made the rules?

If the universe runs on a rule, who wrote it?

Wolfram's idea Nobody. In his picture, which he calls the ruliad, every possible rule runs in every possible way, all at once, all tangled together. Our universe is not one chosen rule. It is the slice of the whole tangle that observers like us are able to see.

His analogy: once 1, + and = are defined, 1 + 1 = 2 without anyone making it true. The rules of physics, he says, are like that. They are simply what is there.

Many rules, all running at once A glimpse of the tangle: each tile is a different rule from scene 2, running on its own.

Why would we see these laws and not others? Wolfram's idea Because of what kind of observers we are: beings who persist through time, who are tiny compared with the ruliad, and who assume the world is one consistent thing. Different kinds of observers would carve out different laws.

Caveats. It is a hypothesis. Critics argue that Wolfram treats everything that is mathematically possible as physically real, which is a leap, not a measurement. And it does not settle "why is there anything at all?" for everyone: religious and philosophical answers to that question differ, and they are not the kind of thing a ball count can decide.

Make a guess

Who wrote the rules of the universe?

There is no scoreboard for this one. Wolfram's idea is the third option: nobody, because every rule is already "there", the way 1 + 1 = 2 is there. Whether that counts as an answer, or just moves the question, is exactly what his critics debate.

Scene 8

The honest part

What the model explains, what it hopes to explain, and what it has not touched.

Make a guess

What share of the universe is ordinary matter: the atoms in stars, planets and you?

About 5%. The rest is dark matter, unseen stuff that pulls on galaxies (about 27%), and dark energy, whatever is speeding up the expansion of space (about 68%). Any theory of everything has to account for all three.
