Kitimat, British Columbia · Rio Tinto BC Works

Aluminum is electricity you can hold

At the head of Douglas Channel, a line of several hundred steel boxes turns falling mountain water into about 420,000 tonnes of metal a year. This is a walk through how it works, from a reservoir on the Nechako plateau to the ships at the wharf.

Stop 01 Energy in your hand

You can't mine aluminum

Aluminum is the most common metal in the Earth's crust, but you'll never find a lump of it. It's always locked to oxygen, and that bond is so strong that no fire or furnace will break it the way charcoal breaks iron ore apart. The only practical way is to tear the oxygen off with electricity.

That takes a lot of it. A modern smelter uses roughly 14 kilowatt-hours for every kilogram of metal. So every aluminum object is, in a real sense, stored electricity. Pick one:

Stop 02 Water, mountains, sea

Why build a smelter in a fjord?

A smelter needs three things: an enormous, steady supply of cheap electricity, a deep-water port to bring in raw material and ship metal out, and room. In 1950 Alcan found all three on B.C.'s north coast, and then built the electricity itself.

The trick was to turn a river around. The Kenney Dam on the Nechako River flooded a chain of lakes into a single reservoir on the inland plateau. Instead of flowing east to the Fraser, that water was sent west, through a 16 km tunnel bored straight through the Coast Mountains. At the far end it drops about 790 m down steel penstocks to the Kemano powerhouse, which is carved into the mountain itself. An 82 km transmission line then climbs over Kildala Pass to Kitimat.

Each pot draws about 1.7 MW (roughly 400,000 amps at about 4.2 volts). The smelter uses most of Kemano's output, and Rio Tinto sells the surplus to BC Hydro.

Who paid for the water The reservoir flooded the homes, traplines and graves of the Cheslatta Carrier Nation, who were moved off their land with only days of notice in 1952. Flows released down the Nechako are still contested. The smelter itself sits in the traditional territory of the Haisla Nation, whose village of Kitamaat lies across the bay.

Stop 03 From rock to white powder

Kitimat never sees the ore

Aluminum starts as bauxite, a reddish tropical clay mined in places like Australia, Guinea and Brazil. Refineries near the mines crush it, dissolve the aluminum part in hot caustic soda, filter out the iron-rich red mud, then crystallize and roast what's left. The product is alumina: aluminum oxide, Al2O3, a white powder like coarse flour. This is called the Bayer process.

Ships bring that alumina across the Pacific to Kitimat's wharf. The smelter's whole job is the second step: splitting alumina into aluminum and oxygen.

Stop 04 The pot room

Inside a pot

The process every smelter on Earth uses was invented in 1886, independently, by Charles Martin Hall in Ohio and Paul Héroult in France. Both were 22. Their trick was to dissolve alumina in molten cryolite, a sodium aluminum fluoride. Pure alumina melts above 2,000 °C, but in cryolite it dissolves at about 960 °C.

That molten mix, called the bath, sits in a steel box lined with carbon. Big carbon blocks hang down into it from above. Current flows down from those blocks (the anodes), through the bath, and out through the bottom. Aluminum collects on the floor as a liquid pool, and the oxygen leaves by burning the anodes:

2 Al2O3 + 3 C → 4 Al + 3 CO2

    Two things in that model surprise most people. First, the pot is heated by its own electricity. The bath resists the current, and that resistance turns into the heat that keeps 960 °C of salt molten. Second, the pot controls itself through its voltage. When alumina runs low the voltage creeps up, and the control computer reads that as a cue to feed. Let it run too low and the voltage jumps from about 4 to 30 volts or more. That's an anode effect, and it releases perfluorocarbons, gases thousands of times more potent than CO2.

    Stop 05 Counting electrons

    Metal out is set by current in

    Each aluminum atom needs exactly three electrons to go from ion to metal. Michael Faraday worked out the rule in the 1830s: the mass you make is proportional to the charge you push through. So a pot's output is fixed by its current, and its energy bill by current times voltage.

    kg per day = I × 86,400 s × 26.98 g/mol ÷ (3 × 96,485 C/mol) × efficiency

    Current efficiency is the share of electrons that actually make metal. Some of the aluminum dissolves back into the bath and gets re-oxidized by CO2, wasting the charge that made it. Good pots run around 94–96%.

      Stop 06 The potline

      Hundreds of pots on one wire

      Pots are wired in series, like old Christmas lights. The same 400,000 amps flow through every pot in turn, and their voltages add up. Rectifiers at the end of the line convert the grid's AC into one enormous DC circuit. Kitimat's modernized potline has somewhere between 360 and 384 pots, depending on which of its builders you ask, in long buildings that add up to more than a kilometre. The whole line runs at well over 1,500 volts.

      Series wiring has a catch: if one pot fails, the whole line goes dark. So each pot has heavy shorting bars that crews can wedge in to bypass it while the current keeps flowing. Click pots in this small 40-pot line to cut them out.

      All that current makes strong magnetic fields. Steel tools tug in your hand, and older pot rooms were known for stopping wristwatches. The fields also push on the liquid metal and set up slow waves in the pool. If a wave touches an anode, it shorts the pot and wastes energy. Much of modern pot design, including the AP40 cells at Kitimat, comes down to routing busbars so the magnetic forces cancel out and the metal stays flat.

      Stop 07 The rest of the plant

      A factory that feeds the pots

      The pot rooms get the attention, but a smelter is really a ring of plants feeding them. Anodes burn away and have to be replaced every few weeks, so a smelter runs its own carbon factory. Fumes from the pots are cleaned using the alumina that's about to go into them. And the metal has to be cast into shapes customers can use.

      Stop 08 The carbon question

      The power source decides the footprint

      Every smelter emits some CO2 directly, because the anodes burn. That's about 1.5 tonnes per tonne of aluminum, and no amount of clean electricity removes it. But the electricity itself is a much bigger number. At 14 MWh per tonne, a smelter on a coal grid emits roughly ten times more than the anodes do.

      This is why Kitimat's aluminum is among the lowest-carbon in the world: its power comes from its own hydro system. The chart uses the energy figure from your pot calculator in Stop 05.

      Stop 09 What comes next

      Pots that breathe out oxygen

      The last big source of direct emissions is the carbon anode. Rio Tinto and Alcoa's joint venture ELYSIS is developing inert anodes made of materials that don't burn. The oxygen leaves as oxygen gas instead of CO2:

      2 Al2O3 → 4 Al + 3 O2

      Without anodes to replace, a smelter wouldn't need its carbon plant or its anode-changing crews. The chemistry needs a bit more electricity, though, so it only pays off where that electricity is clean, as it is at Kitimat.

      The other half of the answer is already here. Remelting scrap takes about 5% of the energy of making new metal, and aluminum can be recycled indefinitely. Roughly three-quarters of all the aluminum ever made is still in use.

      So the drink can from Stop 01 holds a long story: rain on the Nechako plateau, falling 790 metres inside a mountain, carried over a pass on high-voltage wire, pushed through 960 °C salt by a few hundred thousand amps. Recycle it, and most of that work never has to be done again.
